Problem Description – Stroke
Overview
Stroke occurs when blood flow to a portion of the brain is restricted. 
 Ischaemic – restriction from a blood clot
Haemorrhagic – restriction from artery bursting
Portion of the brain dies 
Huge societal costs – 4% of total health care costs in industrialised world (Donnan et al., 2008)

Impact
Approximately 152,000 times a year in the UK
Over 1.2 million stroke survivors in the UK.
Stroke is one of the largest causes of disability.
Over a third are discharged requiring help with ADL’s.




--slide 4
Problem Description – Stroke
Impairments
Wide range of disabilities 
Hemiparesis – weakness down one side of the body
Affects 70-85% of first stroke sufferers
67% of patients still had difficulties with upper extremity movement four years post stroke (Broeks et al., 1999)
Can be treated with physiotherapy – expensive
Patients don't get enough therapy – need affordable robotic devices 

Hand Specific Impairment
Flaccidity 
Weakness
Spasticity 
High tone

3. Aim
Design, build and test a functional and portable robotic device that would aid in post stroke rehabilitation of the flaccid/stiff hand by providing active assisted therapy to patients with impairment while monitoring progress via a user-interface. 
--slide 10
3. Objectives
Undertake research in order to understand the principles of strokes and their consequences, clinical need, current treatments and therapies, as well as wearable robotic devices and bio-mechatronics.
Research literature and produce an analysis of hand motion including range of motion, degrees of freedom, force and grips required, etc.
Formulate a design specification using the information gathered from the literature.
Follow a design cycle to generate a conceptual design on CAD that would enable patient to perform three ADL’s.
Design an initial control system to power the device and feedback user interface
Finalise the design, manufacture and assemble a prototype comprising of:
Hardware: An exoskeleton with comfortable materials suitable for varying hand sizes.
Software: User-interface that would provide visual feedback along with a control mechanism that assessed progress and altered delivery of rehabilitation method accordingly.
Develop a testing mechanism to ensure the efficient functioning of the device.
Produce necessary risk assessment documentation coupled with a user guidelines

3. Design Development
Middle, ring and pinky fingers.
All three fingers are actuated using a servo motor and closed loop wire systems.
Coupled joint motion for both flexion and extension.
Wire transmission guided using centre of rotation of the joints.
Hyper-flexion/extension prevented through length of the close loop wire transmission.
Suitable for a range of hand sizes.




--slide 11
3. Design Development
Index Finger
MCP and PIP joints are actuated by means of servo motors and closed loop cable transmission.
Torque produced is sufficient to actuate a flaccid hand
ROM for the MCP is 80°and for the PIP is 90°.
Mechanical stops prevent hyper-extension/flexion
Adjustable to a range of hand sizes

A HELPING HAND: ROBOTIC REHABILITATION AFTER STROKE
Marwan. Ahmed, Huynh M. T. Le, Amritha Leo, Gabriela A. Noe Ponce, Stephen Worsley
Abstract
Every year, stroke affects nearly 15 million people worldwide. The majority of survivors are left with some level of weakness in the upper limb and hand. Rehabilitation of hand motion is crucial for patients to be functional in daily life. A shortage of trained physiotherapist has created an opening for development of robotic rehabilitation devices. This study presents the design and development of a robotic rehabilitation prototype. A preliminary analysis of hand motion and forces was conducted to inform the design.  The proposed device was designed to allow grasping and pinching motions, with emphasis on precise motion of the index finger. A control system was implemented successfully, actuating the index finger for passive assist therapy. Furthermore, computational testing on the mechanical structure of the device was performed to inform material selection and design optimization for future development.  Additionally, functional testing of the index finger actuation and design was performed to validate the device. Lastly, implementation of the control system to the remaining four digits is the target of future work on the proposed prototype. 
Keywords — Stroke, flaccid hand, robotic rehabilitation
1. Background
Stroke is a clinical syndrome affecting over 150,000 people per year in the United Kingdom alone . Aside from having a high mortality rate, strokes are the leading cause of long term disability in Europe. The neurological effects of stroke on survivors range from decrease or loss of motor and sensory skills to impaired vision, language and cognitive skills . Psychological effects such as depression have also been reported in up to 68% of all cases . From the neurological effects, hemiparesis, a neurological deficit causing weakness or partial paralysis down one side of the body, is the most common. 
Hemiparesis of the upper-limb is reported to affect approximately 85% of stroke survivors and persist even five years after the stroke occurre. Hemiparesis of the upper limb, particularly of the hand, presents itself as flaccidity or spasticity. Spasticity is characterised by high muscle tone and activity, and velocity-dependent resistance to passive muscle stretch. For the hand, this presents as a tight fist or stiff fingers. Whereas, flaccidity is characterised by muscle weakness and loss of dexterity. Some studies have reported that spasticity had only occurred in 19% of the post-stroke patients when compared to flaccidity, during the sub-acute and chronic stages . 
To further understand the effects of stroke, and understanding of hand anatomy is required. The anatomical structure of the hand is composed of the carpus making up the wrist and root of the hand and the five digits, which are labelled from one to five starting from the thumb. Digits 2-5 are made up from three bones named the proximal, medial and the distal phalanges. For these digits, the joints between the distal and middle phalanges are named distal interphalangeal joint (DIP) and the joint between the medial and proximal phalanges is called proximal interphalangeal joint (PIP). A third joint is located between the proximal phalanx and the metacarpal bone called metacarpal-phalangeal joint (MCP). The thumb differs from the rest of the digits as it does not have a medial phalanx, and hence only has an MCP joint and one interphalangeal (IP) joint. All IP joints are hinge joints, capable of only flexion and extension. The MCP joints are condyloid joints and are capable of flexion/extension, adduction/abduction and also a small degree of rotation. Each digit also has a carpometacarpal (CMC) joint where the metacarpals meet the distal carpal bones. For the thumb this joint is a saddle joint capable of flexion/extension, abduction/adduction and rotation unlike for the other digits which provide stability to the hand as appose to mobility.
Hand function is considered one of the most important in daily life. Hand motion rehabilitation of stroke patients is commonly treated with physiotherapy. It has been shown that repeated motor practice of motions used in activities of daily living (ADLs) encourages a positive recovery outcome . The most common functional hand motions include tip pinch, palm pinch, lateral pinch, cylinder grip, spherical grip and power grip . It has also been shown that the amount of physiotherapy received directly affects the level of recovery. Treatment usually takes place in hospital during the acute stages, and at home or at rehabilitations centers once the patients have been discharged during the chronic stage . Due to the existing shortage of physiotherapists and the continuously increasing number of stroke patients requiring rehabilitation, there is a need for an alternative treatment method.
Over the last decade, robotic hand rehabilitation devices have introduced as an alternative to physiotherapy to increase the quantity of therapy available to patients. Robotic rehabilitation devices can be classified based on their overall mechanical design as end-effectors or exoskeletons. End effector devices are those which only interact with the most distal part of the hand and tend to have simple structures. However, end-effectors increase the complexity of control when multiple degrees of freedom (DoF) are involved. Exoskeleton based devices are those with a structure mirroring the skeletal structure of the hand. These devices permit more precise motion control since all segments associated with a particular joint movement have a matching segment or component in the exoskeleton structure . Robotic devices can also be categorised based on the type of assistance they provide. Active assist devices are those allowing the user to perform the targeted motion and regulating the level of assistance based on the user’s performance. Passive assist devices are those in which the user remains inactive and the device produces the motion. Both types of assistance are employed by both physiotherapist and rehabilitation devices, however active assistance is encouraged to promote more improvement . The most common actuation methods in hand rehabilitation robotics are electric actuators and pneumatic actuators. Electric actuators are easy to control and reliable. Additionally, a wide variety of types are available in the market, such as DC and AC motors . Pneumatic actuators are known for having lower impedance and weight when compared to electric actuators. However this does not account for the compressors required to power them . Compressors are generally noisy and large in size, limiting the portability of devices. 
Some of the current challenges facing development of new rehabilitation devices is the need to be portable and low cost in order to be used in a home environment or at rehabilitation centres. The aim of this study is to design, build and test a portable prototype hand robotic rehabilitation device to provide active assistive movement therapy with a graphical user interface to post-stroke patients suffering from hand flaccidity.
2. Hand Motion & Force Analysis 
Functionality and safety were the two essential aspects in this project. Hand motion and force analysis were required to ensure safety for the user and to avoid abnormal hand movement patterns. The findings of this analysis are discussed in the subsequent sections.
2.1. Hand odel and nstraints
2.1.1. Kinematic onfiguration of the hand
A variety of hand models currently employed to perform kinematic hand motion analysis, using between 20 and 26 DoF, were reviewed . In order to achieve a balance between complexity and realism, a hand model with 24 DoF was used in this report . There were two kinematic configurations; one for digits 2-5 and one for the thumb. Digits 2-5 were represented by four links and five DoF . In these fingers, the MCP was modelled by a two DoF universal joint while the CMC, PIP and DIP had one DoF. The thumb was defined by three links and four DoF. The thumb’s CMC joint was modelled by a 2 DoF universal joint whereas MCP and IP joints are modelled by 1 DoF .
2.1.2. Hand Constraints
Hand and finger motion is constrained to a specific range or pattern of motion. The two main constraint types are: Type I static constraints. This refers to the limits of the range of motion (ROM) as a result of the hand’s anatomy. Assuming no external forces are applied and neglecting the thumb, the ROM can be summarized as follows :
Type II dynamic constraints. This type of constraint refers to the limits imposed on the joints during motion and can be divided into intra-finger and inter-finger constraints. The intra-finger constraints are between the joints of the same finger. Based on the hand anatomy. For instance, to flex the DIP joints of the fingers, the PIP joints must also flex. Inter-finger constraints are the ones imposed on the joints between the fingers. For example, flexing the MCP of the index finger will naturally cause the MCP of the middle finger to flex as well.  The same is true for the middle, ring and little fingers.
2.2. Hand otion nalysis
A motion analysis was performed as part of this project to identify the maximum angles and range of velocities that could be achieved by each finger joint. The findings from this analysis alongside literature were used to define the limits of the ROM of the robotic device and select the appropriate actuators in order to ensure user’s safety.
Optotrak Certus and InfraRed Emitting Diodes (IREDs) were used to capture the motion of the hand. A low sampling frequency of 100Hz was used as the signal amplitude of hand motion is maximum below 6Hz . The data recorded was processed using MATLAB. This experimental setup and MATLAB algorithm were validated by using a test rig of various known angles. A maximum error of 2.7° was found, making this method more accurate than a goniometer which is traditionally used to measure joint angles by surgeons and therapists. 
In the ROM and velocity analysis, the index and thumb of four subjects were analysed. IREDs were placed on 3D printed rings on each of the phalangeal segments of both fingers. Subjects were required to perform pinching, abduction, and quick and slow grasps. The definition of slow was left unexplained to the subjects to determine what velocity was considered by people as slow in terms of hand motion. Figure 2.1 illustrates the maximum recorded angle for each joint of the index and thumb. 
The low sampling size and existence of outliers skewed some of the results (thumb MCP, index Abduction & DIP) which was clearly indicated in the sample t-test. However, the values for the MCP & PIP of the index matched the population data (p=0.057 & 0.373). 
Table 2.1 illustrates the results of angular velocity for fast and slow motion. The majority of the results matched the range of velocity found in literature. For simplicity, it was decided to actuate all finger joints of interest with the same velocity. According to these results and literature findings, the suggested velocity for actuation was between 3.00 - 3.79 rad/s .

3. Design
3.1. Design ycle
Pahl’s et al.  design process was selected as the most suitable approach to allow for a systematic engineering approach to find a solution to the project task.
3.2. Requirements and pecifications
A design specification document to inform the design of the device was formulated. It defined the criteria that needed to be met and is summarised below:
The device must be: biomechanically compatible and match the ROM of the hand; lightweight to prevent user fatigue; portable to allow for usage at home and rehabilitation centres; and capable of providing prolonged active assist therapy addressing grasp and pinch. The device must be able to provide rehabilitation safely by preventing hyperextension using mechanical stops and safety features, such as an emergency stop button. The maximum torque applied to the MCP, PIP and DIP must not exceed 2Nm, 0.75Nm, and 0.25Nm respectively and the angular velocity cannot exceed 17.45rad/sec. 
These user requirements were used as guidelines to design the rehabilitation device.
3.3. Conceptual esign 
3.3.1. Abstraction and function structures
The task is defined in generic terms through abstraction, this was done to remove any presumed constraints. The essential problem was defined as: “Provide movement therapy to the hand using a robotic device.”
From the abstracted definition, a function structure was created to highlight the essential functions, to which, solutions must be found. Figure 3.1 shows the system function structure. 
3.3.2. Working rinciples 
Possible solutions to each sub-function were researched and a table of working principles was created. Working structures were then created by combining different solutions to each sub-function. 
3.4. Rejected oncepts
During the conceptual and embodiment design stages, several combinations of working principles were investigated. After evaluation of all produced variants based on safety, layout, ergonomics, manufacturing and cost, a final design solution was found.
3.5. Final esign rototype
The final design can be broken in  modules, namely; the index finger module, digits 3-5 module orthosis and hardware module. These are described in more detail below. 
3.5.1. Index inger Module
An exoskeleton structure has been developed to facilitate independent actuation of the MCP and PIP joints. The structure was mounted to the radial side of the index finger to enable accurate alignment of rotational axes. The structure was made adjustable to accommodate a range of hand sizes. This was achieved by adjusting the relative distances between the MCP and PIP joints, and also the MCP to the dorsum orthosis. Actuation was implemented using DC servomotors with a cables/pulley transmission. Pulleys on the proximal and medial phalangeal sections were connected to pulleys fixed to the servomotor arms in a closed loop using low friction Nylon cables. This configuration enabled both flexion and extension of a joint to be actuated using a single servomotor, reducing both cost and weight. Contact between the cables and the exoskeleton structure has been minimized to reduce frictional losses. For the PIP joint, cables were guided as close as possible to the central axis of the exoskeleton, this ensured the relative distance between the joint and the actuator was constant throughout the ROM. This was considered essential if the closed loop transmission was to work effectively. 
The angular displacement at each joint was determined by measuring the angular position of the servomotor shafts. A simple spur gear transmission, with 2:1 ratio, was used to rotate the potentiometers with the servomotor shaft. This gear ratio was deliberately chosen to increase the accuracy of the position measurement. Force sensing would be achieved using pressure resistive ink sensors placed between the finger and orthosis. However, in the current state, the device does not include force sensing. 
3.5.. 3rd-5th Fingers odule
For this module, independent exoskeletal components have been developed to allow flexion and
extension actuation for all joints, as well as passive abduction and adduction motions about the MCP joint of each finger. The exoskeleton components were mounted on the dorsal side of the proximal, middle and distal phalanges of each finger. The use of independent components ensured adaptability to a wide range of hand sizes. Actuation of this module is performed using a DC servomotor with a similar cable/pulley transmission to the Index Finger Module. Three pulleys of different radii, one for each finger, were mounted on the shaft of the servomotor and low friction Nylon cables were used to create an individual closed loop per finger through the components. The cable guides in each component maintain the cable over the dorsal central axis of each digit. A block component which inserts into the most proximal component, limiting the translation of the cables was implemented as a mechanical stop to prevent hyperextension. The dorsal components were attached to the finger using straps of Velcro to further accommodate for different hand sizes. On the palmar side of each strap, the cables were guided back to the pulley system on the servomotor to enable flexion. 
The motion of the three fingers in this module during both targeted movements (grasp and release and tip pinch) does not change significantly. For this reason, the use of a single actuator with three pulley loops enabled the desire motion using a single actuator, also reducing overall cost and weight. The motor used for this Module was a Bluebird 621DGM+HS DC servomotor able to provide a torque of up to 0.71 Nm. Overall angular displacement for this module was measured through a rotary potentiometer linked to the servomotor. The same type of potentiometers used in the Index Finger Module were used for this Module. Force sensing would be accomplished using readings of motor current and speed from the control module of the prototype. This module can be seen on Figure 3.3.
3.5.. Dorsum and rist rthosis
The orthosis serves three main purposes; to support the index finger exoskeleton structure, to provide support for the motor and position sensing mountings, and to secure the device comfortably to the hand/wrist. The dorsum and wrist orthosis was designed in three sections for development purposes but in detailed design these would be combined in to a single part. The orthosis has been designed with a 20° angle to hold the wrist at an angle which is approximately in the middle of the functional range. 
4. Control
4.1. Hardware Development
4.1.1. Electronic system
An electronic system was developed for the exoskeleton to implement a passive-assist control mode. This mode was to provide full assistance to the user’s hand while performing flexion and extension of the fingers. This required designing a closed loop controller to control the angular position and velocity. Therefore, the usage of angular position sensors was critical in providing position feedback. The electronic hardware system was divided into three units: a central control, an actuation, and position sensing unit. Figure 4.1 shows a diagram of the electronic system with respect to the system function structure. Based on the electronic system diagram, a list of hardware components was used in the project.
4.1.2. Components used
A field-programmable gate array (FPGA) was used for its high performance illustrated in its capability of running multiple commands In addition, the National Instrument Compact Reconfigurable Input/Output (NI cRIO) platform was used.  The NI cRIO combines embedded real-time technology providing an advantage in obtaining real-time data from the angular position sensors. Two C series modules, NI Digital Input/Output (DIO) 9401 and NI Analog Input (AI) 9215 module were added onto the  NI cRIO 9024 controller. As shown in Figure 4.1, the NI 9041 module was programmed in FPGA environment to Pulse Width Modulation (PWM) signal to the MCP and PIP motors respectively. Meanwhile, the NI 9215 module continuously acquired voltage data from the potentiometer of both MCP and PIP motors at a sampling rate of 20 ksps for channel 0 and 10 ksps for channel 1. The power supply unit (PSU) was used to power the servomotors and potentiometer at 6V and 5V respectively. Due to design constraints, the size of the potentiometers and the servomotors were to be minimal. Therefore, the Vishay P11P potentiometer, the Power HD 2688HB (MCP motor) and Corona Digital Servo DS-929MG (PIP motor) were chosen.

5. Testing
5.1. Material election test
In order to meet the design requirement of a lightweight and durable device, a material selection test was performed. The material was chosen based on two analyses; a finite element analysis (FEA) test and a cost per unit volume analysis. 
Titanium alloy, aluminium alloy, ABS and PLA were included in the material selection test as they were commonly employed in previous rehabilitation robotsAlso, PLA was used to manufacture the device owing to popularity within 3D printing. 
Aluminium alloy had a density of 27700Kg/m3and experienced a maximum stress of 75.773Mpa, which was under the endurance limit of aluminium alloy (86.2MPa). Hence, aluminium alloy was chosen as it met the design requirements and had a successful clinical history .
5.2. Computational esting
To confirm functionality, a deformation test was performed and, to confirm safety and reliability, a stress analysis was performed. These tests were conducted using finite element methods as it was a cost-effective method and provided solutions in a quick and efficient manner. 
The components that were directly in contact with the fingers were tested because failure within these components would be a safety hazard to the patient. The individual component was tested under conditions of flexion and extension. Whereas, the exoskeleton was tested under different joint angles.
5.2.1 Individual omponent
Upon completion of the FEA tests on the individual component, the device experienced a high level of stress within the baseplate. This stress was transmitted to the bottom of the baseplate and proved to be a hazard for the patient, as shown in Figure 5.1.
Owing to these stress concentrations, the device was optimised, changing the frame of the baseplate from a rectangular component to a cylindrical componentThis adaptation resulted in an evenly distributed stress map that would avoid stress concentrations on the bottom surface of the plate, as shown Figure 5.. Furthermore, the width of the curved component was reduced to make the device more lightweight and reduce material costs. Also, the regions that experienced stress concentrations were filleted to permit an even stress distribution. 
These changes resulted in a stress reduction of 66%, therefore the maximum stress that this component would experience 70.432MPa under extension and 306.91MPa under flexion. Additionally, the maximum deformation experienced was 0.041mm. This was proven to fall within an acceptable range, as a safety factor of 2.204 was predicted. These findings suggested that the design would meet its design life of 1 billion cycles and not experience failure due to fatigue.

5.3 Regulatory Considerations
Medical devices are directly related to the health and safety of a patient, therefore, it is governed by certain regulatory bodies. Unfortunately, as robotic rehabilitation is a developing field, standards exclusive to medical robots are currently unavailable. Hence, the hand rehabilitation robot adheres to standards of similar application. The material chosen was in accordance with ISO 10993. It was identified as a low risk material as only surface contact of under 24 hours will be experienced. Additionally, Aluminum alloy has previously been employed in rehabilitation robotics. Therefore, it can gain approval via the 510(k) premarket approval.
 Robot rehabilitation devices were classified as IIa within Europe and as class II within the U.S. allowing a structured risk analysis procedure. 
Additionally, a failure modes effects and analysis (FMEA) was performed on the device. Four major hazards were identified; hyperextension of the fingers, breaking the joints, electric shock and mechanical failure of the device. Hyperextension can be prevented via the use of potentiometers and mechanical stops embedded in the design of the device. Furthermore, an emergency stop button was integrated in the control system. 
The device was designed to address flaccidity, therefore the motors employed produced low torque values. Hence, this hazard was deemed negligible. Also, to compensate for the electrical shock, the device was tested following the BS EN 60601-1:2006. Lastly, finite element methods were tested using an over predicted load. The device passed the structural testing. Therefore, the hazards were deemed negligible.

5.4.2 Potentiometer ensing est
Tests were conducted to assess the accuracy and suitability of the potentiometer and gearing combination. The Optotrak motion capture system and IREDs were used in the same way as in the index finger testing. The angular position was also measured using the potentiometer. Two test subjects were used for the test, however, due to erroneous results, only one test subjects data could be analysed. Each test subject was instructed to fully extend their hand before forming a fist and then opening their hand again. Hand motion was measured at 100Hz using both the Optotrak system and the potentiometers. This process was repeated three times for each subject. NI LabVIEW and a myDAQ was used to acquire voltage data. This voltage was then converted to angular position at the joints. The potentiometer data was incorrectly calibrated to 0° during the tests resulting in erroneous angular position data. This was corrected in MATLAB by removing the offset between the Optotrack and potentiometer data, thus allowing useful information to still be gathered. 
The potentiometer data and the Optotrak data was then plotted together over time. The results from the three trials were all very similar. Figure 5.7 shows the results from trial 2. As can be seen the potentiometer for both the MCP and PIP joint tends to match the Optotrak data very closely during flexion. However, the measurements start to deviate when the velocity decreases to zero or the direction changes. 
It is clear from igure 5.7 that the error increases over the duration of the test. This is further illustrated in igure 5.8 where the error between the Optotrak data and potentiometer data is shown over time. It can also be seen that the potentiometer measurements are constant for a brief period when the velocity is zero or when the direction changes. From igure 5.8 it is clear that the error between Optotrak and potentiometer data has increased significantly over the grasping motion.
The results from this test shows that there are problems with this method of sensing. The period, when the potentiometer reading is constant, is probably a result of excessive backlash in the gears. This results in the measurement drifting significantly (approximately 4°) over the grasp-release cycle. This is not suitable as this error would compound over multiple cycles and thus accurate control would not be possible. A possible solution to this would be to use gears with a smaller module so that backlash is reduced.

A. Problem Overview
Context
Stroke is the second most common cause of death worldwide and a major cause of disability in adults. It poses a huge problem to society with treatment accounting for 4-5% of the total health care costs in the industrialized world (Donnan et al., 2008). As the population ages and survival rates increase, more people are living with disabilities resulting from stroke (Sivan et al., 2014). The range of disabilities is vast but one of the most common is hemiparesis (weakness down one side of the body). Between 70-85% of first strokes result in hemiparesis (Dobkin, 2004) which leads to limited mobility and difficulty completing basic activities of daily living (ADL’s). 
Physiotherapy is often used to treat hemiparesis. Treatment will usually focus on neurofacilitation techniques, task-specific training and task-oriented training (Schaechter, 2004) with the aim of reducing impairment and improving functional use. The problem with traditional methods used to treat hemiparesis is that they are expensive and also labour intensive. As a result, patients often forego the required treatment – limiting functional recovery. In addition the treatment will often stop once the patient leaves hospital. Studies have shown that recovery is proportional to the intensity of treatment (Kwakkel et al., 1997), (Nijenhuis et al., 2015). In addition some studies suggest that functional recovery may also be made in chronic stroke patients with prolonged muscular flaccidity (Formisano et al., 2005). As such the need for rehabilitation devices that would enable the intensity of treatment to be increased and also enable patients to use at home would be extremely beneficial. 
A prototype of a low cost portable device that will provide therapeutic exercise to the hand is to be developed. This will help further the effort being made to develop affordable robotic devices that aid the recovery of hemiparesis in patients who have suffered a stroke.

1. Introduction
This document presents the RFP that all Level 4 project teams will employ in the production of their specific Contract Performance Plans (CPP). This document should be read alongside the MECH5030M module documentation.
Stroke is one of the largest causes of disability in the uk with approximately 152,000 strokes occurring each year. The outcomes of stroke are wide ranging, affecting upper limb movement, gait, and cognitive ability amongst others. Around 77% of survivors suffer from a level of movement deficit in the upper-limb, this weakness in the arm and hand can result in a lack of functional dexterity. Impaired fine motor skills make it difficult to engage in activities of daily living such as eating or dressing. Physiotherapy aims to assist recovery after stroke through the principle of motor learning, whereby patients aim to regain lost function by undertaking frequent, repetitive, task-specific exercise. Robotic systems are seen as a method of improving patient outcome by delivering addition exercise, however most systems focus on gross arm movements but ignore the function of the hand which is key for undertaking key activities of daily living.
The aim of this project is to develop a reliable, low cost and portable prototype of a wearable robotic device for the rehabilitation of hand. The robot will assist stroke patients to undertake hand manipulation exercises as part of their rehabilitation, promoting the recovery of hand function. 
By using assistive robotics and haptic feedback technology it is possible to guide a patient’s hand such that normal patterns of movement are promoted. As the patient improves, it should be possible to reduce the assistance to allow the patient to develop their own independent movements.
The team will design a mechanism that will attach to a patients hand and provide some kind of assistive force to allow practice of appropriate movement patterns. These appropriate movement patterns may be derived from literature or through use of motion capture technology. Analysis of this data will inform a detailed specification of the system, including the required speed of actuation, accuracy and range of motion. 
The team will then look at appropriate actuation methods and create a concept design suitable for manufacture. Software will be developed in LabVIEW and tested on the manufactured device to ensure safe accurate control of the system.
The outcome of the project will be a prototype robotic system that can assist an adult with stroke to undertake standardised exercise tasks. An accompanying user interface will provide the user with task instructions and performance feedback.

• Robotic devices aim to increase the amount of therapy available to these patients to 
regain movement and be able to perform activities of daily living.
• Analysing hand motion using Optotrak and MatLAB 
allowed us to identify the range of motion and the 
constraints of the hand. 
• This will be used to set the requirements for the 
robotic device and ensure its safety.
• Our measurements included flexion and extension 
angles of the joints of the index finger and thumb 
as well as angular velocity of a grasping movement.
